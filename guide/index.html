What a WISP actually is — and what your firm should do with it.

A Written Information Security Plan is the written description of how your firm protects sensitive customer information. For tax and accounting practices, it should not be a document that sits untouched in a folder. It should match the real people, systems, vendors, risks and safeguards in the business.

Why tax and accounting firms are hearing so much about WISPs

Tax professionals routinely handle Social Security numbers, income records, bank details, business financials, identity documents and other highly sensitive information. The IRS has repeatedly told tax professionals to create and maintain a Written Information Security Plan and points firms to Security Summit guidance and the FTC Safeguards Rule.

The important idea is proportionality: the program should be appropriate to the size and complexity of the business, the nature of its activities, and the sensitivity of the information it handles. A two-person tax office does not need the same program as a national firm, but “small” does not mean “no program.”

A WISP is a program, not just a template

A template can be useful as a starting structure. The problem begins when a firm assumes that possessing a document is the same as operating a security program. A useful WISP should describe what is actually happening inside the business and should be supported by evidence.

For example, if the WISP says all remote access uses MFA, the firm should be able to verify that configuration. If it says employees are trained annually, there should be training records. If it says backups are tested, there should be evidence of a successful restore test. The written plan and the real-world safeguards should agree.

The core pieces of a practical WISP

1. Clear ownership

Someone needs to be responsible for implementing and maintaining the information security program. The FTC uses the term Qualified Individual. In a small firm, that may be an owner, employee, or qualified outside provider, but responsibility still needs to be clear.

2. A written risk assessment

The firm should identify reasonably foreseeable risks to sensitive customer information, evaluate the safeguards already in place, and decide what additional controls are needed. This is what turns the WISP from generic policy language into something tailored to the actual practice.

3. An inventory of sensitive information and systems

Know what information the firm collects, where it is stored, where it is transmitted, what devices and applications are involved, and who has access. Cloud tax software may reduce some local storage risk, but it does not eliminate risks involving email, laptops, identities, browser sessions, shared drives, remote access or vendors.

4. Access controls and MFA

People should have only the access they need. Access should be reviewed periodically, and accounts should be removed promptly when staff leave or change roles. MFA should protect email and other relevant systems used to access taxpayer information.

5. Device and technical security

Endpoints should be patched and protected, sensitive information should be encrypted where appropriate, and the firm should have enough logging or monitoring to identify suspicious activity. The exact tools will vary with the practice.

6. Training and staff procedures

Employees and seasonal workers need recurring security awareness training. Tax firms should pay particular attention to phishing, impersonation, fraudulent document-sharing requests, password handling, remote work and the handling of taxpayer records.

7. Backup and recovery

Critical data should be backed up in a way that supports actual recovery. Periodic restore testing matters because a backup that cannot be restored is not much of a recovery control.

8. Vendor oversight

Payroll systems, tax software, cloud storage, email providers, IT companies, document portals and other service providers may receive or access sensitive customer information. Relevant providers should be evaluated, and appropriate security obligations should be documented in agreements where required.

9. Retention and secure disposal

The firm should know how long sensitive information is retained and how it is securely disposed of when no longer needed. This includes paper records, old hard drives, retired computers, removable media and cloud data.

10. A written incident response plan

Do not wait for a compromised mailbox or stolen laptop to decide who calls whom. A written response plan should establish responsibilities, internal and external communications, documentation, reporting steps, remediation, and a post-incident review.

11. Testing, review and continual improvement

The program should be reviewed as the firm changes. New employees, new cloud systems, new vendors, a move to remote work, security incidents and emerging threats can all change the risk picture. Testing and documented review help show that the WISP is being maintained rather than forgotten.

What should you be able to show if someone asks, “Is this really in place?”

  • A current WISP with a review date and named responsible person.
  • A written risk assessment and inventory of important systems/data locations.
  • MFA and access-control evidence.
  • Endpoint protection and patching reports.
  • Backup and restore-test evidence.
  • Training records for employees and seasonal staff.
  • Vendor due-diligence and relevant security agreements.
  • A written incident-response plan.
  • Records of periodic review, testing and material updates.

How often should a WISP be updated?

There is no useful “write it once and forget it” model. The IRS emphasizes maintenance and adjustment as the size, scope and complexity of the practice changes. The FTC similarly expects the information security program to remain current in light of changes to operations, risks, personnel and threats. A practical firm should establish a recurring review cycle and also review the plan after material changes or security events.
